A Question You Can Measure

Most bad analysis starts before any data is touched. "Are we losing customers?" has no population, no time window, and no decision attached, so two analysts can answer it with different numbers and both be right. A measurable spec fixes the argument before the work starts.

The five parts of a spec

  1. The decision. What someone will do differently depending on the answer. If nothing changes, the question is curiosity, not analysis.
  2. The population. Who or what is counted — all customers, active accounts, first-time buyers — and who is excluded, with the reason.
  3. The time window. A start and an end, aligned to the decision, not to the calendar.
  4. The metric and its definition. The formula, the numerator, and the denominator, written down.
  5. The threshold. What result would change the decision. This is the part teams skip, and the part that stops a report from becoming decoration.

Why definitions cause disputes

Two teams report churn of 4% and 9%. Neither is lying. One counts cancellations in the month they happen; the other counts accounts that were active last year and inactive now, including accounts that never renewed. Until the definition is written, the argument is about words, not customers.

Check the data can answer it

A spec is only useful if the data exists at the required grain. Ask: is the field recorded, at what level, for the whole window, and with what known gaps? Discovering that the region field was added in March is cheaper now than after a dashboard ships.

Write it before you query

Five lines in a shared document saves a week of rework: decision, population, window, metric, threshold. The analyst who writes it can defend the number; the manager who approves it knows what they are getting.

Try this

Take a question your team asks every month and write the five lines. Then ask a colleague to state, from your spec alone, what result would cause them to act. If they cannot, the threshold is missing or vague.
