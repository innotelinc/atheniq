The One-Page Decision Memo

A report exists to support a decision. If the reader finishes it and does not know what to do, the analysis is not finished — no matter how good the chart is.

The structure

  1. Decision. The question being answered, in one sentence.
  2. Recommendation. What you would do, stated first.
  3. Evidence. Three to five findings, each with its number and its source.
  4. Uncertainty. What the data cannot tell you, and how confident the estimate is.
  5. Options. The realistic alternatives, including doing nothing.
  6. What would change our mind. The observation that would reverse the recommendation.

Uncertainty without hedging

"Sales might go up or down" is not uncertainty; it is refusal to analyse. Useful uncertainty names the range and its cause: "the estimate is 12–18% because two of the five regions have incomplete March data". That sentence tells the reader what to fix.

Charts that survive a screenshot

  • Label the axes and the units.
  • State the denominator in the caption.
  • Do not truncate the axis without saying so.
  • Put the comparison in the same chart, not in a different slide.

When someone asks for one number

Give the number and one sentence of scope: "14%, for the six-month cohort, before the March data correction". A number without its definition will be reused in a context where it is wrong. The memo is what protects both the analyst and the decision.

Try this

Rewrite a report you have received into the six-part structure in one page. If the "what would change our mind" section is empty, the recommendation is a preference, not a finding — say so.
