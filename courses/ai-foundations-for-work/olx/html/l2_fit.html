Task Fit and the Cost of Being Wrong

"Should we use AI for this?" is the wrong question. The useful question is: what does a wrong answer cost here, and how cheaply can a person catch it? A first-draft agenda and a customer refund decision are both text tasks; only one of them can absorb a silent error.

Three questions before you delegate

  1. Cost of a wrong answer. Annoyance? A rework loop? Money, safety, or someone's rights? The higher the cost, the narrower the use.
  2. Reversibility. Can the output be withdrawn before it acts — before it is sent, filed, paid, or used to decide something about a person?
  3. Cost of checking. If verifying the draft takes longer than doing the task, the assistant is not saving work; it is moving it.

The three tiers

TierUse it forGate
Use nowLow-cost, reversible, cheap to check: brainstorming, reformatting your own notes, a first outlineYou read it
Use with reviewCustomer-facing drafts, summaries that inform a decision, code or formulasA named person checks facts and commits
Do not useDecisions about people, safety-critical steps, anything requiring data you may not paste, work that must be attributableNot delegated

Fit changes as the task changes

A task is not permanently in one tier. "Summarise this public report" is use-with-review. Add a customer's complaint letter and it becomes restricted-data work. Remove the review step because the team is busy and a use-with-review task becomes an unreviewed one — which is the failure mode that actually causes incidents.

Worked example

A support team wants AI to draft replies to billing questions. Wrong answers cost money and trust; the output is reversible only before sending; checking a short reply against the billing system is cheap. That is use-with-review: the assistant drafts, an agent verifies the account facts and sends. Now change one variable — the assistant is allowed to issue refunds directly. Cost and reversibility both move, and the task leaves the delegated set entirely.

Try this

List ten tasks from your own week. For each, write the cost of a wrong answer, the reversibility, and who would check it. Put the three highest-cost tasks at the bottom of the list and notice how little of the week is left in "use now".
