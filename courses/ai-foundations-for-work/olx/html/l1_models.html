Prediction, Not Knowledge

Every AI tool in this course is a prediction engine. It has read an enormous amount of text and learned which words tend to follow which. When you ask it a question it does not look anything up, check a database, or know whether it is right. It produces the most plausible continuation — fluently, at speed, and without a signal that says "I am guessing here".

What that explains

Behavior you seeWhat is happening
A confident, well-written answerPlausible text is exactly what the model optimises for
An invented citation or statisticConfabulation: the shape of a fact without the fact
The same question answered differentlySampling: the output is not deterministic
Detail that drifts over a long chatEach turn is conditioned on what is in the context window
Advice that sounds like your policyIt has read policies — not your policy

Why fluency is not evidence

Humans use fluency as a shortcut for credibility: polished prose usually means someone thought about it. A language model breaks that shortcut, because polish is free and correctness is not. The skill this course builds is simple to state and hard to keep: separate how the answer sounds from what supports it.

Consider a draft memo that says "our refund window is 45 days, per policy 4.2". The sentence is grammatical, specific, and cites a document. None of those properties make it true. It is true only if policy 4.2 says so — and the way to know is to open policy 4.2.

What the model is genuinely good at

  • Drafting a first version you will edit anyway.
  • Reformatting material you supply: notes into an outline, an outline into an email.
  • Re-explaining one idea at a different level or in a different structure.
  • Generating options — five openings, ten practice questions — where you pick and verify.

Notice the pattern: the model works on material you bring, and the judgment about what is true stays with you.

Try this

  1. Ask a tool for three facts about a process at your own workplace, with sources.
  2. Check each fact against the source it names. Record which ones survive.
  3. Ask the same question again in a new chat. Note what changed.

Most learners find at least one confident claim that does not survive the check. That experience — not a warning slide — is what makes the verification habit stick.
