Passwords, MFA & Phishing

Most breaches begin with a stolen credential. The strongest defenses are good passwords, multi-factor authentication, and a user who can spot a phishing message.

Passwords

  • Length beats complexity. A long passphrase is stronger and easier to remember than a short jumble.
  • Unique per site. Reuse means one breach compromises every account.
  • Use a password manager. It generates and stores strong, unique passwords.
  • Never share a password, including with IT. Support never needs a user's password.

Multi-factor authentication (MFA)

MFA adds a second proof beyond the password. In strength order:

  1. Hardware security key (FIDO2) — phishing-resistant.
  2. Authenticator app with a number match or push.
  3. One-time code by app or SMS (SMS is the weakest).

When a user loses their MFA device, verify their identity through the approved process before re-enrolling. Never bypass MFA on the strength of a phone call.

Recognizing phishing

  • Unexpected urgency: "your account will be closed today".
  • A sender address that is close to, but not exactly, the real domain.
  • Links whose visible text and real destination differ — hover to check.
  • Requests for credentials, payment, or a code read aloud.
  • Unexpected attachments, especially invoices and "scanned documents".

If a user clicked

  1. Disconnect the device from the network.
  2. Reset the affected password immediately from a known-good device.
  3. Report to security; a fast report limits the blast radius.
