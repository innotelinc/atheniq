The Process You Actually Run

Every automation project starts with a document that says how the work is supposed to happen. The useful map is the one that says how it actually happens — including the workaround, the exception, and the step nobody can explain.

What to capture

  • Trigger. What starts the work: a request, a schedule, an alert, a person noticing something.
  • Steps. What is done, in order, by whom. Include the manual lookups and the message threads.
  • Handoffs. Where work waits for another person. Most delay lives here, not in the steps themselves.
  • Decisions. The points where someone exercises judgment, and what they use to decide.
  • Exceptions. The cases that do not follow the path — and who handles them.
  • Failure points. Where it currently goes wrong, how often, and what it costs.

Find the steps that exist by habit

Ask why each step exists. "Because the old system needed it" and "because a manager asked in 2019" are common answers. A step that exists only to satisfy a system nobody uses is a candidate for removal before any AI is involved — removing work is cheaper and safer than automating it.

Choose the first step to assist

Prefer a step that is frequent, bounded, text-heavy, and checked by a person who already does the check today. Avoid starting with the decision point, the exception path, or the customer-facing commitment. The first assisted step should make the team's week easier without changing who is accountable.

Map with the people who do the work

A map drawn by management is a hypothesis. Walk the process with the person who runs it and you will hear about the spreadsheet, the phone call, and the workaround that the official document omits. Those are exactly the places an automation will break.

Try this

Map one process you own, end to end, on a single page. Mark the three steps most likely to break if an AI did them, and the one step you would assist first. Bring the map to the classroom — the peers will find an exception you forgot.
