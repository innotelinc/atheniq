A Pilot That Tells the Truth

Pilots fail in a predictable way: they measure the thing the sponsor wants to hear. "Saved the team time" and "everyone liked it" are not evidence. A useful pilot has a baseline, honest measures, a stopping rule, and a handover.

Before: baseline

Measure the current process for two weeks: time per case, rework rate, error rate, customer response time, and volume. Without a baseline, any number afterwards can be claimed as an improvement.

Measures that include the cost

  • Time saved in the step — and time added in review.
  • Rework caused by the new output.
  • Error rate that reached a customer or a system of record.
  • Coverage: what share of cases the workflow actually handled.
  • Exceptions: how often a person had to take over.

The net gain is time saved minus review and rework. A workflow that saves ten minutes and creates fifteen minutes of review is a loss, however good the demo looked.

The stopping rule

Write it before the pilot: "if error rate exceeds the baseline, or rework more than doubles, we stop and revert". A stopping rule agreed in advance is what lets a team report a failure honestly.

Handover

A workflow is not delivered until someone else can run it:

  1. Runbook — the steps, the checks, the escalation route.
  2. Owner — the named person accountable for its output.
  3. Training — how new staff learn the review step.
  4. Rollback — how to return to the manual process, tested once.

Try this

Write the pilot plan for your workflow: baseline, three measures, stopping rule, and handover pack. Then show it to the person who will run it. If they cannot say what they would do when the tool is down, the handover is incomplete.
