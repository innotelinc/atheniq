Drafts, Promises, and Disclosure

Customer-facing work carries the highest consequence per word. A reply can create a commitment, reveal a fact that should not be shared, or escalate a situation that was calm — and it does so in public.

The three risks

  1. Invented facts. Policies, prices, dates, and delivery times that sound authoritative and are wrong.
  2. Unapproved commitments. "We will fix it by Friday" is a promise the organisation now owns.
  3. Tone and blame. A technically accurate reply that reads as dismissive makes the next conversation harder.

What to check before sending

CheckSource
Account facts, balance, datesThe owning system
Policy and eligibilityThe policy document
Any promise of time, money, or outcomeThe person accountable for it
Tone for a frustrated customerRead it as the customer would

Disclosure

Where the employer requires it, say that AI assisted the draft. The useful version is specific and brief: "Drafted with AI assistance and checked by [name]". A vague "this may have been generated" protects nobody. Where disclosure is not required, the standard is still accuracy and a human owner.

Escalate, do not improvise

Escalate when: the customer threatens legal action, mentions a safety issue, asks for a policy exception, is abusive, or the value exceeds your authority. The assistant will happily draft a response to any of these; the decision to answer belongs to a person with the authority to commit.

Try this

Take two AI-drafted customer replies: one that invents a policy, one that is accurate but rude. Revise both, then decide which of the two you would have caught under time pressure. That answer tells you which check your workflow needs.
