Where AI Already Touches the Work

Before a manager can govern AI use, they need to see it. In most teams it is already there: a draft email written with a chatbot, notes summarized on a phone, a personal account used because the approved tool was slow. The first job is a map, not a policy.

The four portfolio questions

  1. Value. What work does this use actually save, and for whom?
  2. Risk. What happens when it is wrong, and who is affected?
  3. Capability. Can the team check the output reliably, and does it know how?
  4. Accountability. Whose name is on the output if it goes wrong?

Plot every known use on value versus risk. The grid tells you where to act first, which is rarely where the loudest enthusiasm is.

Shadow use is a signal, not a sin

People use unapproved tools because they are trying to do the job. Treat the discovery as information: the need is real, the route is wrong. A stop order without an approved alternative moves the behavior further out of sight.

The first decision: stop, standardize, or study

SignalDecision
Restricted data in a public toolStop now; offer the approved route
Low risk, high value, no ownerStandardize: approve a tool and name the check
High value, unclear riskStudy: a bounded pilot with measures
High risk, low valueStop; the benefit does not justify the exposure

What the manager owns

Not the model, and not the vendor relationship alone. The manager owns the decision about which work may be assisted, who checks it, and what evidence would change the decision. That is a management responsibility, and it can be delegated only with the authority to stop the work.
