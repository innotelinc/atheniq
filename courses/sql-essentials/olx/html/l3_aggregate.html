Aggregating & Joining

Two ideas do most of the work in real reporting: collapsing many rows into a summary, and combining tables that share a key.

Aggregates

  • COUNT, SUM, AVG, MIN, MAX — one value per group.
  • COUNT(*) counts rows; COUNT(column) counts non-null values in that column. They differ, and the difference is often the answer.
  • COUNT(DISTINCT column) counts unique values — "how many learners", not "how many enrolments".

GROUP BY

Every column in the SELECT that is not inside an aggregate must appear in the GROUP BY. Filter the groups afterwards with HAVING, not WHERE — WHERE runs before the grouping exists.

Joins

JoinKeeps
INNER JOINOnly rows matching on both sides
LEFT JOINEvery left row; unmatched right columns are NULL
RIGHT JOINEvery right row (mirror of LEFT)
FULL OUTER JOINEverything from both sides
CROSS JOINEvery combination — use deliberately

Choose the join by the question. "List every learner and their enrolment count, including learners with none" needs a LEFT JOIN and a COUNT of the right table's key; a plain INNER JOIN would silently drop exactly the learners you were asked about.

Traps

  • A join that matches many-to-many multiplies rows and inflates every SUM. Aggregate first, or confirm the grain before and after.
  • Putting a condition on the right table of a LEFT JOIN in WHERE turns it back into an inner join — put it in the ON clause to keep the unmatched rows.
  • Aggregating a column that is NULL-suffused: AVG ignores NULLs, which can quietly change the denominator.

Always check the row count after a join against the counts of the inputs. If it grew faster than the grain allows, the join is wrong.
