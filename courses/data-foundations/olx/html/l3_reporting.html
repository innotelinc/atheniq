Reporting & Visualisation

A report answers a question for a particular reader. Decide the question and the reader first; the chart follows from them, never the other way round.

Start from the question

  • Comparison — how do categories differ? Bar chart.
  • Change over time — line chart. Match the interval to the decision, not to what is easy to query.
  • Composition — a part of a whole. A bar or a stacked bar beats a pie chart past three slices.
  • Relationship — scatter plot. Plot the points; a trendline without them hides the shape of the data.
  • Distribution — histogram or box plot. An average alone hides the spread and the second peak.

Charts that tell the truth

  • Start bar charts at zero; a truncated axis exaggerates differences.
  • Label axes with units, and dates in an unambiguous format.
  • One chart, one message. If it needs three sentences of explanation to be read correctly, split it.
  • Use colour to carry meaning or not at all; never rely on colour alone.
  • Show the denominator. "12 failures" means nothing without the total.

Metrics and dashboards

Write the definition of every headline number down: the period, the population included, and the exclusions. A metric that can be computed two ways will be. A dashboard is a small set of stable numbers a person checks, not an everything-page — one row for the headline, one for the drill-down.

Finish with the narrative, not the numbers: what changed, what it means, and what you recommend. The reader is there for the last one.
