Email, Docs, Notes, Sheets

The highest-value AI use at work is rarely a clever prompt. It is the ordinary task you do twenty times a week, done once with an assistant and checked properly every time. This chapter covers the four places that work actually happens.

Email: draft fast, commit slowly

Ask for a draft with the audience, the ask, and the boundary stated. Then check three things: facts (dates, figures, names), commitments (promises of timing, money, or outcome), and tone (does it blame someone who should not be blamed?). Commitments are the costly error: a model will happily write "we will have this fixed by Friday" because it reads as helpful.

Documents: outline first, prose second

Use the assistant on structure before wording. "Turn these notes into a five-section outline with one claim per section" produces something you can correct cheaply. Polishing prose first hides structural problems under good sentences.

Meeting notes: decisions and owners, or it did not happen

Ask for a fixed shape: decisions made, owners and dates, open questions, and anything that was explicitly not decided. Generative summaries tend to smooth over disagreement — the "not decided" list is the part that protects the team.

Spreadsheets: formulas need a test

A generated formula can be syntactically perfect and semantically wrong. Always test it on a row where you know the answer, check what happens with blanks and zeros, and prefer a formula you can read over one you cannot. For anything financial or safety-related, have a second person review the formula, not just the result.

The review gate

OutputGate
Internal emailRead it; check facts and commitments
External emailSecond reader for anything with a promise or a price
Policy answerCheck the policy document itself
Spreadsheet formulaTest on a known row; peer review if it drives money
Meeting summaryOwner confirms decisions and dates

The gate is not a formality to be skipped when the day is busy. It is the reason the assistant is allowed to draft at all.

Try this

Pick the office task you repeat most. Write the brief once, save it, and use it for a week. At the end, record the time saved and the errors caught. Both numbers belong in the case for keeping the workflow.
